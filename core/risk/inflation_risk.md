# Inflation Risk

## Definition

Inflation risk is the possibility that the general rise in prices will erode the purchasing power of a retirement portfolio faster than anticipated, causing future withdrawals to buy less than expected. It is the risk that your money will not stretch as far as planned over a multi-decade retirement.

## Why It Matters

Inflation compounds silently. At 3% annual inflation, $1 in today's purchasing power becomes $0.41 in 30 years. For FIRE retirees with 40–50+ year horizons, even small miscalculations in expected inflation compound to massive shortfalls. A retiree who underestimates inflation by 1% annually will find their real income 25–30% lower than planned by year 30.

## Mathematical Framework

### Purchasing Power Erosion

```
Real Value = Nominal Value / (1 + inflation_rate)^years
```

### Inflation-Adjusted Withdrawal

```
Withdrawal(t) = Withdrawal(0) × (1 + inflation_rate)^t
```

Over 30 years at 3% inflation:
```
Withdrawal(30) = $60,000 × (1.03)^30 = $60,000 × 2.427 = $145,620
```

Your annual spending more than doubles just to maintain the same lifestyle.

### Real Return Requirement

```
Real Return = Nominal Return - Inflation Rate (approximate)
```

If your portfolio earns 7% and inflation is 4%, your real return is only 3%. Over 30 years:
```
Real Growth Factor = (1.03)^30 = 2.427×
```

vs. the nominal 7% expectation:
```
Nominal Growth Factor = (1.07)^30 = 7.612×
```

The gap between nominal and real growth is 3.13× — a massive difference.

### Inflation Risk Premium

To maintain purchasing power, a portfolio must earn returns above inflation:
```
Minimum Required Return = Inflation Rate + Withdrawal Rate
```

At 3% inflation and 4% withdrawal: minimum 7% nominal return required just to avoid drawing down principal in real terms.

## Historical Inflation Examples

### US Hyperinflation (1979–1980)
- Annual inflation exceeded 13%
- A retiree withdrawing $40,000 in 1979 needed $88,400 by 1981 just to maintain the same purchasing power
- Many fixed-income retirees were devastated

### Lost Decade (2000–2010)
- Low nominal inflation (2.5%) but combined with low investment returns
- Real returns were near zero or negative for conservative portfolios
- Purchasing power barely maintained despite "low" inflation

### Post-COVID Inflation (2021–2023)
- US CPI hit 9.1% annual in June 2022
- Retirees on fixed withdrawals saw immediate purchasing power cuts of 15–20%
- A $60,000 withdrawal in 2020 had the purchasing power of ~$48,000 by mid-2022

### Stagflation (1970s)
- High inflation (6–14%) combined with low/negative real returns
- Traditional 60/40 portfolios failed to keep pace
- Illustrates the danger of simultaneous high inflation and low real returns

## Hedging Strategies

### 1. Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities (TIPS)
- Principal adjusts with CPI
- Guarantees real return if held to maturity
- Limitation: yields may be low; liquidity risk in early retirement

### 2. I-Bonds (Series I Savings Bonds)
- Interest rate = fixed rate + inflation rate (based on CPI)
- Tax-deferred; $10,000/year purchase limit
- Excellent for cash reserves; illiquid for first 12 months

### 3. Real Assets
- Real estate (REITs, direct ownership) tends to appreciate with inflation
- Commodities provide direct inflation exposure
- Infrastructure investments with inflation-linked contracts

### 4. Equity Allocation
- Stocks historically outpace inflation over long periods (average real return ~7%)
- Companies can pass cost increases to consumers
- Limitation: short-term volatility; not reliable for near-term spending

### 5. TIPS Ladder
- Construct a 5–10 year TIPS ladder
- Each rung matures to cover one year of expenses
- Guarantees inflation-adjusted income for the ladder period

### 6. Dynamic Withdrawal Adjustment
- Monitor actual inflation quarterly
- Adjust withdrawals dynamically rather than using fixed assumptions
- Build 1–2% inflation buffer into initial withdrawal calculations

## Examples

### Example 1: The silent destroyer
- **Portfolio**: $1.5M, **Withdrawal**: $60,000/year (4%)
- **Year 1**: $60,000 buys 1,000 "units"
- **Year 10** (3% inflation): $60,000 buys 744 units (26% less)
- **Year 20**: $60,000 buys 554 units (45% less)
- **Year 30**: $60,000 buys 412 units (59% less)

Without inflation-adjusting withdrawals, real spending declines by 60% over 30 years.

### Example 2: Underestimating inflation
- **Planned**: 2.5% inflation → FIRE Number of $1,500,000
- **Actual**: 3.5% inflation
- **Real shortfall at year 30**: Purchasing power is 23% lower than planned
- The $1.5M portfolio provides $46,000/year in real terms instead of $60,000

### Example 3: TIPS as a hedge
- Retiree holds 40% in TIPS (600K), 60% in equities (900K)
- During 2021–2023 high inflation: TIPS adjusted upward by 8–12%
- Equity portion remained volatile but provided long-term growth
- Combined portfolio maintained purchasing power better than a nominal bond portfolio

### Example 4: Geographic inflation arbitrage
- US retiree moves to Portugal (lower local inflation)
- Expenses: $40,000/year vs. $60,000/year in the US
- Lower expenses = smaller FIRE Number = less inflation exposure
- Currency risk mitigated by maintaining some USD-denominated assets

## Mitigation Strategies

1. **Plan with conservative inflation assumptions** — Use 3–4% rather than recent lower estimates
2. **Maintain equity exposure** — At least 50–70% in equities for long-term inflation protection
3. **Hold TIPS and I-Bonds** — 20–40% of fixed income in inflation-protected securities
4. **Build an inflation buffer** — Target FIRE Number with 15–20% above calculated minimum
5. **Diversify internationally** — Geographic diversification hedges against US-specific inflation
6. **Include real assets** — REITs, commodities, and infrastructure provide inflation hedging
7. **Reassess every 3–5 years** — Update inflation assumptions and FIRE Number as data changes
8. **Maintain withdrawal flexibility** — Ability to increase withdrawals during high inflation periods

## Relevance to FIRE Planning

Inflation risk is the most persistent and underestimated risk in FIRE planning. Unlike sequence risk (which is concentrated in the first 5–10 years) or longevity risk (which can be mitigated with flexibility), inflation compounds relentlessly across the entire retirement horizon. A FIRE plan that does not explicitly model and hedge inflation risk is incomplete and likely to fail in real terms.
